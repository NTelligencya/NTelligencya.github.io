Talks & sessions

Presentations

Short, self-contained presentations on cybersecurity, AI literacy and digital skills topics; 30 minutes to an hour each. They run entirely in your browser: no PowerPoint, no downloads, no installs. Use the arrow keys or swipe to move through the slides; press one into service for your own team session, or just read it over a coffee.

01 Using AI in Excel: From Copilot Chat to Add-ins

A practical guide for a general audience: how to use free Copilot chat as a formula tutor, why large language models struggle with grid-structured data and hallucinate confidently, what the Claude and ChatGPT Excel add-ins actually do, and how the same AI can turn your analysis into a PowerPoint deck. Runs through the golden rule at every step: verify everything before it counts.

02 How Scammers Fake It with AI

A cyber-safety session built for community audiences, first delivered for the Alice Springs Aboriginal Researcher Network. How scammers use AI to clone voices, fake celebrity endorsements and spoof websites; the "platform switch" red flag; and the fake online casino ("scambling") scams targeting NT communities right now, with the current national figures cited in full. Closes with Stop, Check, Protect and where to get real help, including services for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

03 Australia's AI Laws: What's Coming

An explainer for non-lawyers and students, prepared July 2026. On 15 July 2026 the Prime Minister announced "Australian Standards for AI", a single mandatory national framework with legislation intended for early 2027. This deck covers the surprising baseline (Australia has no dedicated AI Act, unlike the EU), what the laws seem likely to cover (artists' control of their work in AI training; sovereignty; data centre rules on location, power and water), why technology ends up needing legislation at all, and the civics of how a press release becomes a law. Every quote is from the published address, with sources cited in full.

04 Is This a Scam? Ask an AI

An interactive session teaching one practical habit: when a message feels off, copy it, paste it into an AI assistant like Claude or ChatGPT, and ask "does this look like a scam?". Two hands-on scenarios run live in the deck; a fake Australia Post "parcel held" email, and the "Hey Gran, I lost my phone" voicemail, each pasted into a simulated AI chat that spots the red flags and explains them. Closes with why scam-checking is a task AI is reliable at, its honest limits, and how to word the question well.

05 Putting a Website Online, Explained

A plain-language walkthrough of how a website actually gets published, for anyone who has found the process baffling. It separates the job into four understandable pieces: the domain name you rent, the host that serves your files, the DNS that connects the two, and the HTTPS certificate that locks it. Built around an interactive "follow one visitor" trace that steps a single request from a typed address through DNS to GitHub and back, and it explains the usual sticking points; public versus private repositories, DNS propagation, and the "not eligible for HTTPS yet" wait, so they read as normal rather than broken. Uses GitHub Pages and Namecheap as the worked example.

06 MFA and Account Security

A short cyber-safety session on why multi-factor authentication matters, for a general and workplace audience. It explains in plain terms how passwords get out (reuse, phishing and bulk breaches), the three kinds of proof a sign-in can ask for; something you know, something you have, something you are, why an authenticator's number-matching prompt is safer than a simple tap-to-approve, and what happens, step by step, when an attacker who already has your password runs into that second factor. Closes with a push you did not ask for as a warning sign, and four everyday habits that do most of the work.

07 Mermaid: Diagrams You Write Instead of Draw

Most diagrams are drawn, which is why most diagrams are out of date; the cost of a small correction is too high, so small corrections never happen. This walkthrough introduces Mermaid, the small text language that renders as a diagram, for anyone who needs a flowchart or a site map and either has no drawing licence or is tired of maintaining pictures. It teaches the flowchart syntax through a live editor built into the page, so you type the text and watch the diagram redraw, then covers where Mermaid already renders (a live editor, GitHub, note applications, VS Code), how to ask an assistant to write it and what to check in what comes back, and closes with an honest comparison of when to write a diagram and when to draw one.

08 Are You a Robot? CAPTCHAs Explained

Everyone has squinted at the letters, ticked the box and hunted for the buses; almost nobody has been told why. This walkthrough covers where verification tests came from, the book-digitising trick hidden inside reCAPTCHA, what the tick box is really reading while you look at it (not your mouse movements, as it turns out), and the published research showing machines now pass these tests faster and more accurately than people do. It carries a strand on the surge in automated traffic through to the mid-2026 crossover, when bots passed humans as the majority of requests, and closes on what is being built to replace the squiggle. Six working recreations are built into the page: distorted text, the tick box, the nine tiles, the invisible score, the slider puzzle, and a proof-of-work challenge that genuinely runs in your browser. An advanced module then turns the same tool around, on darknet markets and forums where the adversary being designed against is a researcher with a scraping pipeline rather than a spammer; eight documented examples, shown as screenshots by permission of DarkOwl. It closes on the pre-release evaluation in which GPT-4 hired a TaskRabbit worker to solve a CAPTCHA for it, told the worker it had a vision impairment, and was believed; quoted from OpenAI's own system card, with the caveats the popular retellings leave out.

09 How Email Works

Email is the oldest thing most people use every day, and the only major internet service still running on a design set out in August 1982. This opens a message up: the header block and who wrote each line, the two separate "from" addresses that the protocol allows to disagree (the gap almost every impersonation attack lives in), how a photograph is cut into labelled parts and rewritten as plain letters to fit through a pipe built for English text, and the eight-step journey from send button to inbox. It also corrects the common story that email is "broken into packets and reassembled in your inbox"; packets are real, they just belong two layers down. Ends by handing straight over to the Email Header Forensics Lab.
