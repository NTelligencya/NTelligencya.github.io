The Price of Thinking:
AI Subscriptions and the New Digital Divide

The best AI tools now sit behind subscriptions that cost more than a phone plan. This hour is about who gets access, who gets left out, and what that does to education and opportunity.

Prices and access programs in this deck were current when researched in 2025; both change often, so check the linked pricing pages for today's numbers.

KEY IDEAa divide with three layersThe third divide
The idea that frames this hour

Researchers warn of a third digital divide

First divide

Devices and connection

The gap everyone knows: who has a computer and internet access at all. Still unresolved in much of the NT.

Second divide

Skills to use them

Access alone isn't enough; digital literacy decides who can actually turn connection into capability.

Third divide

Advanced AI + instruction

The new gap: those with premium AI tools and quality instruction in using them, versus those with technology access only; or none at all.

Why it compounds: students at wealthy institutions don't just get better AI tools; they get guidance on ethical use, curriculum integration and critical AI literacy. Everyone else navigates alone, or is excluded entirely.

WORKAROUNDShow the underfunded copePooling power
What resourceful institutions are doing about it

Buying together beats buying alone

·Consortium buying: programs like Internet2's NET+ help institutions pool resources for enterprise AI licences instead of negotiating solo.
·System-wide deals: state university systems negotiate agreements across every campus at once, using scale to move the price.
·Means-tested access: some experts propose full premium AI free for students on financial aid; the same logic as existing software discount programs that once spread Microsoft Office and Adobe through education.
The precedent: premium software becoming a standard, subsidised academic resource has happened before. The open question this hour keeps returning to: should AI access be treated like a library database; just part of what an institution provides?

EVIDENCEtechnology alone never fixed itThe human factor
The evidence from the last big experiment

COVID's lesson: access without humans isn't enough

What happened

Devices didn't close the gap

During the pandemic, even where devices and internet were universal, achievement gaps persisted. The hardware arrived; the outcomes didn't follow.

What mattered

Human support did

Research from Ghana, Indonesia, Poland, Saudi Arabia and the United States found parental and tutor involvement remained crucial for learning outcomes, regardless of the technology available.

Applied to AI: handing out subscriptions is the easy half. The third digital divide is about tools plus instruction; equity programs that fund licences but not teaching support are repeating the pandemic's mistake.

THE GOOD NEWSAI built to teach, not just answerLearning modes
The encouraging development

The big providers now ship learning-optimised modes

OpenAI

Study Mode

Guides students through problems step by step with Socratic questioning: "How would you approach this?", "What evidence supports your conclusion?"; understanding before answers.

Google

Guided Learning

Gemini breaks complex topics down, adapts to the student, pulls in diagrams and videos, and generates personalised flashcards and study guides from course materials.

Anthropic

Claude for Education

Learning mode within Projects develops independent thinking through guided exploration; core concepts and structured templates rather than direct answers.

The pedagogy is real: these modes draw on established research, Socratic method and adaptive learning theory. The stated goal is capable, independent thinkers; not AI dependency.

THE CATCHread the fine printLimits & freebies
Before anyone declares the problem solved

Two catches, one loophole

·Same engine underneath: learning modes still run general models trained on internet data, not curated educational materials; the tutoring is a layer, not a different brain.
·The escape hatch: students can switch back to the regular chat interface any time; the pedagogical guardrails are optional, and everyone knows it.
·Student freebies exist but expire: OpenAI offered US and Canadian students free ChatGPT Plus through 2025; Google has offered students a free year of AI Pro in several countries. Generous, time-boxed, and unevenly distributed by geography.
The equity angle: discount programs concentrated in North America don't do much for a student in Tennant Creek. Watch what's offered here, not what's announced in a US press release.

TRUE STORYeducation by WhatsAppSOLAx
A true story about doing more with less

After the Taliban banned secondary education for girls in 2021, the number of Afghan girls openly attending secondary school fell from 1.1 million to zero by 2024. SOLAx, co-founded by Mati Amin, rebuilt a school inside the one piece of technology those girls still had: WhatsApp.

A smartphone and a basic connection is the whole entry requirement. Daily, asynchronous coursework arrives in three languages (English, Dari and Pashto); families can enrol several children from a single shared device; and the platform reaches girls inside their homes. Where sophisticated learning platforms fail on poor connectivity, a messaging app succeeds; innovation that works within constraints instead of wishing them away.

CLOSER TO HOMEwhat SOLAx teaches the TerritoryNT lessons
Reading SOLAx from the Northern Territory

Design for the technology people already have

·Content over interface: SOLAx prioritises delivery over polish, so it works in low-bandwidth environments; the same trade-off suits remote NT communities where connectivity is thin and data is dear.
·Shared devices are a feature: designing for one account per household, not per person, matches how technology is actually used in many communities.
·Repurpose existing platforms: partnering with Meta to use WhatsApp's API beat building an app nobody could download; which familiar platforms could carry education and services in remote Australia?
The proof of concept: SOLAx is both immediate crisis relief and a demonstration that low-tech delivery can carry serious education. Equity solutions don't have to start with premium subscriptions.

FRAMEWORKwhat a real strategy coversFour dimensions
What a serious equity strategy has to cover

Digital equity has four dimensions, not one

Digital literacy

Skills, including critical AI literacy

Teacher training and community programs; the ability to evaluate AI outputs, understand bias, and use AI strategically rather than as a shortcut to thinking (Digital Promise).

Affordability

Who can pay, and who shouldn't have to

Bulk licensing, consortium deals, means-tested access; treating cost as a policy problem, not a fixed fact.

Culturally sensitive content

Built in from the outset

Culturally relevant content, multilingual support, respect for different learning traditions and knowledge systems; one-size-fits-all platforms quietly exclude.

Infrastructure

The unfinished first divide

Connectivity and devices still gate everything else; in the NT this dimension is nowhere near solved.

The systems lesson: the research is consistent that these four move together; and the European experience suggests coordinated national strategies beat fragmented institution-by-institution fixes.

DISCUSSIONover to the roomThe open question
For discussion

Should access to AI tools be treated as a basic academic resource; like the library, the databases, the journals; rather than a premium add-on for those who can pay?

And if so: who pays, in a landscape where a 500-seat licence costs as much as several teachers' salaries? What should public policy do to keep AI access from widening achievement gaps across Australian communities; and what would a Territory-shaped answer look like, given the SOLAx lesson that the best delivery platform is the one people already have?

TAKEAWAYthe hour in five linesTakeaways
The takeaway: five things to remember

The price of thinking, summed up

·The third divide is tools plus instruction; premium AI access with guidance versus free tiers without it compounds existing advantage.
·Cost is the gatekeeper; enterprise AI runs to hundreds of thousands a year, and it's the number-one reason institutions opt out.
·Humans remain the active ingredient; every study says support and teaching matter more than the technology's sophistication.
·Learning modes help but don't solve; they're a welcome layer over general models, and students can switch them off.
·Constraint breeds design; SOLAx reached students through WhatsApp; equity in the NT may look less like subscriptions and more like meeting people on the platforms they already use.
