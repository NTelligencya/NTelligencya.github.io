// finding your way

Teams, channels and chats

Teams holds two kinds of conversation, and telling them apart clears up most of the early confusion. A chat is a private message to one person or a small group. A channel is a shared space that belongs to a team, where posts stay in the open for everyone in that team to see and reply to.

The rule of thumb: a quick word with one person goes in a chat; anything the whole team should see or be able to find later goes in a channel. Files shared in a channel live in that team's shared library, so they are still there when the conversation has scrolled away.

// joining a meeting

Joining, and finding the controls

Most Teams meetings arrive as a calendar invitation with a Join button; click it near the start time, check your microphone and camera on the screen that appears, and join. A first meeting holds no surprises once you know what the screen looks like.

The controls you actually need sit in one bar: mute and unmute your microphone, turn your camera on or off, share your screen, open the chat, raise your hand, and leave. Everything else can wait until you want it.

// hosting a meeting

Scheduling, chairing and hosting

Setting a meeting up is a short job: in the Teams calendar, New Meeting, give it a clear title, add the people, pick a time, and send. The invitation carries the Join link for everyone.

Chairing well is mostly small habits: start on time, say what the meeting is for in a sentence, keep an eye on the chat for questions, and use the participant list to mute a noisy line or see who is in the room. As host you can also start a recording, which is where consent comes in.

Recording and consent

Before you start a recording, tell everyone the meeting is being recorded and give them the chance to agree; the same applies when Teams Premium is producing a transcript or an AI recap. Take particular care with sensitive meetings, such as performance or disciplinary conversations, where recording may not be appropriate at all. When in doubt, ask first and follow the council's guidance.

// presenting a deck

Presenting in a Teams meeting

There are two ways to put a presentation on the screen. Share Screen shows whatever is on your monitor, which is simple and works for anything. PowerPoint Live shares the deck itself, which is tidier: your notes and the upcoming slides stay on your screen while the audience sees only the slide, and people can move through it at their own pace if you let them.

A few things make presenting land: share the right thing rather than your whole desktop, close anything you would not want on screen, and pause after each slide so people on a slow connection can keep up. You will put this to work later in the course, presenting the orientation deck you build during the homework week.

Present the deck, not your inbox

PowerPoint Live shares only the presentation, so a message popping up in the corner does not go out to the room. If you use Share Screen instead, close your email and chat first.

// transcripts, recaps and minutes

From a recap to usable minutes

This is where Teams earns its keep. With Teams Premium, a recorded meeting can produce a transcript, an AI recap of what was discussed, and a list of suggested action items, in a couple of minutes rather than an afternoon of writing.

The habit that makes it safe is simple: the recap is a draft, not the record. Read it, correct anything it has misheard or misattributed, and only then does it become the minutes. AI recaps are quick and genuinely useful, and they still get names, numbers and decisions wrong often enough that a person has to check.

It helps to know how that transcript is made, and why it slips on names or in a noisy room. This short walkthrough shows how speech-to-text and handwriting recognition work, and where your file travels to be processed.

Copilot prompt · turn a recap into minutes

Turn the meeting recap below into tidy minutes, with a short summary and a list of action items showing who is doing what and by when. Use first names or roles only. Flag anything that reads like a decision so I can check it. Here is the recap: [paste the recap]

Why it works: it sets the shape of the minutes, a summary plus actions with owners and dates, keeps identifiers to first names or roles, and asks Copilot to flag decisions so you verify them. The minutes are yours once you have checked them, not before.

Once you have the actions, they need somewhere to live so nothing slips. Planner turns them into a shared board with owners and due dates, which beats a list buried in an email.
